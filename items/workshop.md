# Writing fictional uses

**An item is a thing a character owns, not a move they make.** It spends most of its life in a pocket with no cards on the table, and that is where players reach for it first. The card has no room to list those uses, so the Form line must *generate* them: write the object's **job, not its appearance**. "A splitting wedge of black stone" is an appearance; "what it is driven into fails the moment it is trusted" is a job — and the job produces the fictional uses, the mechanic and the Marks alike.

**The Effect is a translation, not a definition**: what the job looks like when the obstacle happens to be a check. Design in that order — decide what the object does in the world, ask what that is against a check, write it in README's vocabulary. Inventing a mechanic and hunting for an object to wrap it in produces every failure below.

## Three tests

- **Three uses.** Name three things you could do with it in a scene with no cards out. If you cannot, it is a move in a costume.
- **Consequence.** State the Effect as a one-sentence consequence of the Form. *A thread anchored at one end drags back what the other end holds* → set a card aside, tug it back.
- **Swap.** Could the Effect sit on a different object unchanged? Could the Wake charge any item in the set? Then the connection is decoration.

When fiction and mechanic disagree, **move the mechanic**. The image is the durable half.

## Slots

| Slot | The rule |
|---|---|
| **Form** | One sentence: one physical detail, one thing wrong. Present tense, no history, no mechanics. Nothing is *eerie*, *cursed* or *strange* — the unsettling part is a flat fact. **Scope limits live here**, not in a rules clause: what stops "it gives way" becoming "the castle falls" is a wedge you have to drive in by hand, one seam at a time. |
| **Wake** | Must be refusable — the GM can say "not here, not tonight" for a fictional reason. An action with a place and a moment, never a handling instruction. Costs something outside the card economy. Repeatable in form, scarce in substance: `whisper-jar` needs a genuinely new secret every time. If the item has charges, the Wake is the charging. |
| **Effect / Cost** | The Cost continues the image; it is not a tax. If you could swap the status without touching a word of fiction, the card has a number instead of a price. Say the trick the fiction promises — `red-thread` spells out the void. Say nothing else: README's preamble owns the global rules, and long cards overflow. |
| **Mark** | Fiction only — never a mechanic, trigger or condition. A condition, not an event. Inward Marks say what it did to you; **outward Marks say what others can see**, and heavy items need at least one, because that is how stigma reaches the table. The Mark should earn the Cost above it. For real social weight, use a Curse compulsion in a Price. |
| **preview** | What the card does, in the card's own terms. Never describe content that is not on the card — that is how `hollow-blindfold` came to advertise a Curse that no longer exists. Rewrite it whenever the lines change. |

Voice throughout: second person, present tense, flat declaratives. No *seems*, *perhaps*, *somehow*. Nothing explains itself.

**A suit is an approach, never a substance.** `RULES.md:38` makes it how you engage — Strength, Dexterity, Intelligence, Weird. Any fiction that treats suits as materials, elements or colours is a category error, and it reads as sense right up until someone asks why burning a wooden door made you less nimble.

## Using an item outside a check

The shared rule lives in [README.md](README.md): invoking an item outside a check still incurs its Cost and any revealed Price.

- **The Wake is spent.** Consume any secret, book, charge, or other resource the use requires, in addition to its Cost.
- **Marks land.** They record having used the thing, not having played a card.
- **Costs fire outside checks.** Apply statuses and fictional consequences when the use resolves, including costs otherwise due at cleanup. Keep stated conditions and explicitly later consequences. Injury gains use the normal between-check survival rules.
- **Check-dependent costs require a check.** If payment needs a hand or a trick outcome, resolve the use as a check; do not waive the cost or borrow a hand from a later check.
- **Charge for invoking the power.** Carrying, inspecting, or ordinarily handling an item does not invoke it.
- **Never bank an unstated mechanical benefit.** Prying a door at noon does not halve a world card that night. An Effect explicitly available between checks still applies as written.
- **The object still needs physical limits.** Costs constrain repeated use; the Form defines what the item can do at all. One wedge stays where it was driven; the page destroys its source; the thread must be attached to its target. A status cost does not explain how much an object can move, reach, or break.

## Failure modes

1. **A mechanic in a costume** — no use in the world, only in a check.
2. **Decoration** — a mood, not a job. Bells that ring before they are shaken point at nothing.
3. **Arbitrary charge** — a Wake unrelated to what it arms.
4. **Cost as tax** — a status with no fictional line running to it.
5. **A Mark with teeth** — flavour implying a rule nobody can adjudicate.
6. **Drift** — an effect written for older rules, re-translated, fiction left behind (`hollow-blindfold`). **When the rules move, re-read the fiction, not just the numbers.**
7. **Preview outliving content.**
8. **Overflow** — trim the Form and the second Mark first, never the Effect's precision.

**Relics carry one extra rule:** the job goes on the **front** card, always. A reveal is secret until it is spent, so anything the player needs in order to use the object in a scene has to be visible from the start. The reveal carries price and consequence only.

## How the trinkets score on use-in-fiction

**Strong:** `red-thread`, `crooked-key`, `slate-shard`, `ripped-page`, `whisper-jar`, `scoped-rifle`, and `borrowed-face` — a lie detector before it is anything else.

**Reopened:** `black-wedge` was rated strong here until it was audited properly. It passes the three-use test only thinly — *destroy a wooden thing* is one verb with three targets — and fails on nearly everything else. Rebuild below.

**Weak:** `grip-bone` — rebuild below. `iron-apple-seed` is flagged twice over: the only Form in the set describing a property rather than a capability, and no limiter beyond being consumed, which is thin even for a consumable.

Relics are audited separately below, and they do considerably worse.

## What the black-wedge pass taught

Five lessons from taking one card apart, each of which cost a round trip to find.

1. **A rating is not a test.** `black-wedge` was called strong twice in this file on the strength of *"the Form generates uses"* — asserted, never run. Actually run, it produced one verb with three targets and failed eleven other checks. Run the tests hostilely or do not record a verdict.
2. **Fixing one guideline can break another.** Making the Wake refusable turned every line inward: the Wake broke your property, the Cost hurt your hand, the Mark rotted your gear. The card satisfied the Wake rule and failed the three-use test worse than the original. **Re-run the whole checklist after a fix, not the rule that prompted it.**
3. **A design note is not a card.** Two rounds of justification rested on a limit — *the wedge must taste a real failure before it works again* — that appeared nowhere in the lines. Same failure as a `previewDescription` outliving its content, and the same failure that lost `hollow-blindfold` its Curse. **If the rule is not on the card, it does not exist.**
4. **Check the fictional justification against a real game concept.** The same-suit cost was defended as brittleness spreading through *the same material*. Suits are approaches. The argument was fluent and meant nothing.
5. **The limiter is a Form question, and it is the one always forgotten.** Every good item in the set already had one and nobody had named the pattern. Ask what stops a spree before asking anything about balance.

# Outstanding

Relics and reveals, plus one shipped trinket sent back.

## black-wedge — rebuild

The shipped card fails the guidelines on eleven counts. The load-bearing ones: its cost's fiction is a **category error** (same-suit spreading is justified as "the same material," but a suit is an *approach*, not a substance — `RULES.md:38`); the Cost's second sentence is a **targeting restriction wearing a Cost's clothes**, and its "it" has no working antecedent; the Effect **ignores the Form's own scope limit** of wood; **trump silently nullifies it**, since a halved card that ruffs still wins on `RULES.md:53`; the Wake costs nothing and **cannot be refused**; and the Wake leaves the tool in a stump while the Effect uses it mid-check.

One number worth keeping: the same-suit restriction was supposed to stop a free shot at the world's best card, but on a fresh 7/6/4/4 deck you hold a given suit **83–97% of the time at N=7** (void: 3.0% / 5.5% / 16.7% for the 7-, 6- and 4-card suits). It was never the decision it was sold as.

**The fix is to commit to the name.** It is called a wedge and the Form calls it a hatchet. A hatchet cuts; a wedge is the tool that makes a thing come apart along a weakness it already had, and it has to be *driven* — which is where the Strength gate stops being decorative. Then move the mechanic from immediate destruction to **latent failure**, which the old Wake already asked for when it said *leave it standing there*.

> *A splitting wedge of black stone, and there is only the one. What it is driven into fails the moment it is trusted.*
> Wake: Drive it into something that will be trusted with weight, and leave it there.
> When: Your approach is Strength.
> Effect: Before a lead, wedge 1 unplayed world card. When played, halve its printed value, rounding down; it cannot be trump.
> Cost: Halve the printed value of your highest Strength card in hand, rounding down.
> *You test your weight on everything now, and you are right to.*

`previewDescription`: "Set into the world's own footing, so the thing it leans on gives at the moment it leans."

**A first pass at this pointed every line inward** — the Wake broke your own property, the Cost hurt your hand, the Mark rotted your gear — and a Form reading *"holds, until weight comes onto it"* states a change of state with no agency in it. That version failed the three-use test worse than the card it replaced. The correction is that **the wedge is a sabotage tool and its fiction belongs on the world, not on you.**

What each change buys:

- **An outward job, in the Form.** *Fails the moment it is trusted* is a capability and it generates real verbs: wedge the bridge so it carries you and drops the pursuit, the door-post so it looks sound to the guard and comes apart on your shoulder, a rope, a ladder rung, a mast, the haft of a weapon someone else is going to draw. The point is not that things break — it is that **you choose when, in advance, invisibly.** That is escape, ambush, delay and framing, which is four verbs where the shipped card had one.
- **The Effect is that same sentence in cards.** The world plays a card at the moment it needs it; the wedge is why it gives. No translation gap at all.
- **`it cannot be trump` is the identity.** A cracked thing cannot take strain. One clause kills the silent nullification on `RULES.md:53`, and it is what finally separates this from `whisper-jar`: the jar changes what a card *is*, the wedge stops a card from *holding*.
- **One wedge, and it stays in what you drove it into.** This is what stops a player wedging every axle, door and weapon in the enemy camp, and it needs no rules clause — *there is only the one* and *leave it there* do it physically. One sabotage is live at a time, you are without the wedge until you go back for it, and pulling it free leaves the thing sound again. That is a real decision rather than a tax, and the Wake stays refusable because you still need access, privacy and something that will bear weight.
- **The cost is exertion, not property.** You set it by hand, with your whole weight. **Highest** rather than a choice is the part doing the work — the shipped cost can be optimised down to a card you did not want, and so could "halve a Strength card." Highest means the better your hand, the more breaking the world costs you: the self-punishing shape `iron-apple` already has.
- **Density.** 491 characters against 519, Form 88 against 123 (set median 72). This card already overflowed export by 22px, so the hedging simile *as though it burned from the inside out* had to go regardless.
- **The Mark stops carrying a capability.** "Everything you grip flakes" was an ability players would try to use. Wariness is a condition, and it earns the cost.

**Known gaps.** Halving *and* stripping trump is a bigger swing than the shipped card, so it may want a heavier gate; the cheapest lever is dropping the halving and keeping only `it cannot be trump`, which is the more distinctive half anyway. Holding no Strength card means you cannot pay and so cannot invoke — standard, but README states it nowhere, and it comes up about 3% of the time at N=7. At 503 characters it is longer than the 491 draft and wants an overflow check on export, though `red-thread` sits at 572 and renders. And the `whisper-jar` overlap is narrowed, not closed: both still answer "the world's best card is a problem."

## grip-bone — rebuild

**The die is never rolled.** The Effect is "name that number before drawing," so the object produces nothing: it is not thrown, its result never matters, and it could be a ring, a coin or a tooth without changing a word. The workshop text that introduced it says *"a die that nobody rolls is just a bone"* and then specifies a die that nobody rolls. Swap test, failed by the item's defining property.

**The Form and the Wake disagree about what was carved.** The Form says the die "always lands on the face you carved"; the Wake carves a number *into your skin*. Those are two different objects being cut.

**The choice has one right answer, always.** The starting deck holds four copies each of 2, 4, 6, 8 and a single copy of 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. Naming is done *before* drawing, so no information can ever make another number correct:

| Named | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copies | 1 | 4 | 1 | 4 | 1 | 4 |
| Expected value gained at N=7 | 3.0 | **10.7** | 2.3 | 8.0 | 1.7 | 5.3 |

Not merely dominant — strictly dominant, with the answer knowable before the game starts. A choice with one correct answer is a formality.

**Smaller faults.** `When: At any time.` is the only card filling the gate slot the template says to omit. *"which is the part that should worry you"* is the narrator winking at the reader, which the Voice rule forbids and which costs 38 characters on the third-densest trinket.

**What survives.** The Mark is the best in the set — outward-facing, socially legible, and it gives the table something to act on. The Injury is exactly earned by the carving. And the limiter is real and already in the Wake: a wound, lasting until it closes.

**The loading is the object.** A first attempt at a fix had the die roll freely and the player carve whatever came up. That inverts the item and destroys it: **the point is that it is loaded, and you are the one who loaded it.** That is also where the fictional use lives — a die that always comes up your number is a con tool, and it works in anyone's hand, so you can throw it, hand it over, or leave it somewhere. Rig the lots for who stays behind, win or lose at dice deliberately, make a decision by "chance" come out the way you need, plant it on someone and let them win until they trust it.

**The fix is the Form, not the mechanic.**

> *A small die cut from yellowed bone. It rolls the number you carved into yourself, no matter whose hand throws it.*
> Wake: Carve a number from 1 to 6 into your skin and close your fist around the die until the cut scabs over.
> Effect: For this check, your cards of that printed value count as 10.
> Cost: Gain 1 Injury.
> *Everyone can read the number on your arm, and people remember which number you were wearing.*

`previewDescription`: "A die loaded to the number cut into your arm, and every card of that value hits like a ten."

- **The contradiction closes.** The Form now reads the number off your skin, which is where the Wake puts it. Nothing is carved into the die.
- **The use is stated, and it is the loading.** *No matter whose hand throws it* is the clause doing the work — it makes the die plantable and usable on other people, which is where the verbs come from.
- **"Name that number" is gone.** The number is carved and visible on your arm, so there is nothing to declare, and the once-per-check timing already comes from README's preamble.
- **The gate slot is dropped** rather than filled with "at any time", and *which is the part that should worry you* — the narrator winking at the reader — is cut. 406 characters against 491, Form 113 against 116.

**Dominance is unresolved, and the fiction is not the place to fix it.** Naming is free and happens with no information, so 2 is strictly correct every time. The only lever that does not touch the object is to let the loading cut both ways — *every* card of that value counts as 10, the world's included, since a loaded die does not care who benefits:

| Named | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| You gain | 3.0 | 10.7 | 2.3 | 8.0 | 1.7 | 5.3 |
| World gains | 6.3 | 5.6 | 4.9 | 4.2 | 3.5 | 2.8 |
| Net | **−3.3** | +5.1 | **−2.6** | +3.8 | **−1.8** | +2.5 |

It does not eliminate the dominance — 2 still leads 4 still leads 6 — but it turns every odd number into an active trap rather than merely a weak pick, and it narrows the spread from 10.7/8.0/5.3 to 5.1/3.8/2.5. It also fits: your number is strong, and strength does not know whose side it is on.

## crooked-key — two locks and retrieval

The old version said the key was too bent to fit a lock, then told you to put it in one. Its Effect returned any card while its preview promised an ordinary card, and its conditional Stumble had no clear relation to opening something. The replacement makes crookedness mean that the key opens a lock by taking a crooked route through another one:

> *A straight silver key that, after turning in one lock, warps as it enters the next; the second stays shut and the first opens.*
> Wake: Turn it first in the lock you need opened, then bring it to a second lock holding someone trapped and turn it there.
> Effect: Return 1 card from your discard to hand, then discard 1 card.
> Cost: Gain 1 Stumble. The second lock is spoiled with the key caught inside. Recover it by dismantling the lock; to wrench it free instead, gain 1 Injury.
> *Two keyways bruise your palm: one clean and open, one crooked and black.*

`previewDescription`: "Leave someone trapped behind one lock to open another, exchanging a card in hand for one you had lost."

The first lock is the inaccessible card; the second lock is the card surrendered from hand. The exchange keeps hand size fixed and allowing statuses makes the key stranger and more useful: it can pull harm back into hand, with the normal draw and survival rules still applying.

Using the key always gains 1 Stumble: transferring an opening through the warped key is the immediate price. Retrieval remains a separate physical problem. The key is unavailable while caught, and dismantling a lock requires access, time and tools the GM can deny. Injury is the shortcut: you may always get the key back now, but the bent metal takes something with it.

The sacrificial lock cannot be a loose padlock or an unattended container. It must hold someone trapped when the key turns. The second lock stays shut and is spoiled, so opening the first lock deliberately prolongs someone else's confinement. Putting the captive in the Wake lets the GM reject a merely valuable lock before the item is invoked and makes every use a concrete bargain rather than an abstract loss.

## grave-sledge — one joining and one repeated action

The Sledge no longer overlaps the Black Wedge's delayed sabotage. It desecrates one named grave to create one impossible fastener:

> *A petrified-wood sledge. A crushed skeleton leaves one bone pin.*
> Wake: Shatter a headstone, exhume the named corpse, and crush every bone.
> Effect: Drive the pin through 2 touching things. It cannot be removed; separating them requires destroying both. The sledge cannot wake while it remains.
> In a check, pin 1 ordinary card from hand face up after drawing. It remains in hand for following suit. Once per trick, if legal, play it without moving it.
> *The same blow lands again and again.*
> Reveal its price.

The effect uses the same verb in fiction and at the table. The physical pin makes one permanent joining of any scale. The card pin repeats one action throughout the check, but continues to constrain follow-suit because it remains in hand. Restricting it to an ordinary card prevents a repeatable Blessed or status exploit.

> Price: When you drive the pin, gain 1 Injury. At cleanup, leave the pinned card beneath the Sledge, outside your deck. It returns to discard only when both joined things are destroyed; then the bone pin crumbles.
> *The sledge and pin yield nothing to the blow. Your body does.*
> *The pin keeps the action that set it. Until the joining is destroyed, it will not give that action back.*

The Injury fires whether the pin is driven inside or outside a check. Repeating a high card can dominate several tricks, but that card then remains outside the deck for as long as the impossible joining survives. The Sledge cannot create a second pin during that time.

## hearthhide-coat — every warning goes quiet

The coat no longer waits for survival. Whenever the wearer relies on Strength, it forces every status out of the hand and replaces those cards one-for-one from ordinary cards already in discard. Survival is included when Strength is the chosen approach, but its recorded cost remains fixed.

> *A coat of scarred hide; beneath it, cold, pain, and exhaustion cannot reach you.*
> Wake: Sleep in it without fire or roof until you stop shaking.
> When: Your approach is Strength.
> Effect: After the deal, set aside every status in hand and replace each with an ordinary card from discard, if available. At cleanup, return them to discard in random order before consequence statuses. The recorded survival cost never changes.
> *The cold stops reaching you, and so does everything else.*
> Reveal its price.

The recurring Price makes ordinary shelter hostile. The first Curse records a taboo against sleeping inside; later invocations add Curses to that same compulsion, increasing the Stress gained whenever the wearer accepts a roof. The outdoor Wake remains compatible with the taboo. If the wearer already has a Curse, the new Curse joins that existing compulsion instead, as the core rules require.

> Price: At cleanup, gain 1 Curse. If you had none, record its compulsion: you may not sleep inside.
> Price: To remove the coat, gain 1 Injury for each Curse not in your reserve.
> *The coat grows into you at the collar, cuffs, and every place your skin has broken.*
> *Removing it tears away whatever has grown through the hide.*

The Curse is the recurring pressure; removal is the real price. The coat survives being cut free, and evolution makes later removal safer by returning Curses to reserve. Other Curses also deepen the price: the hide grows through every supernatural wound the wearer carries, not only the ones it created.

## Curse compulsions are the missing tool

The archive's `hollow-blindfold-price` preview promised "a following thing and a binding Curse: never bare-faced inside buildings." Neither ever appeared in the card's lines.

**An item can record a Curse compulsion**, and the core rules already carry it: one supernatural compulsion, one Stress per Curse outside reserve on every violation, removable only by evolution. The Hearthhide Coat now uses that machinery to turn shelter into a recurring taboo.

## hollow-blindfold — seven tricks through the past

The blindfold circumvents human construction by carrying its wearer through the same place before anything was built there. The card only hints at the truth: impossible pollen, hot wet air, roadless ground, untouched land, three-toed tracks. The wearer cannot see where they have gone and returns before they can participate in the present again.

> *A seamless linen blindfold dusted with pollen from no living flower.*
> Wake: Face a built barrier, put it on, and walk forward without reaching for it. Do not remove it until you mean to return.
> When: Your approach is Dexterity.
> Effect: You may ignore follow-suit, counting as unable, so personal trump stays live. Your first off-suit win raises your goal score to at least its cost; then sit out the rest. Competing goals resolve normally.
> *Behind the cloth, the air is hot and wet, and the ground has never known a road.*
> Reveal its price.

Sitting out gives the automatic score its fictional shape: the wearer has stepped out of the current time and cannot keep taking tricks. The relic is meant to circumvent a difficult check, so its return cost does not scale with the goal bypassed. Returning is always a lone Dexterity check requiring 3 wins from 7 tricks. Consolations remain available.

> Price: Your next check is a lone 7-trick Dexterity check against the world to remove the blindfold and return safely. Your goal costs 3. You return whether you succeed or fail.
> Price: On failure, the GM chooses what fits your return: you appear partly inside something solid and gain 2 Injuries, or a creature follows you back.
> *The land is familiar, but no human hand has touched it.*
> *Three-toed tracks cross your own. Far behind you, something answers with a low call.*

World wins during the return check are encounters with the older landscape and its animals. Failure does not stack Injuries, Stumble, and a following creature. It produces one serious complication: bodily intersection with something that exists in the present, or a living thing crossing back behind the wearer.

## dead-ledger — restore the benefit

The ledger now does something worth wanting outside a check: write the name of someone you truly owe and they forget the debt. They will never bargain with you again. Each use therefore erases a real obligation by permanently closing the relationship that produced it; an invented name or imagined debt does nothing.

The card effect gathers the obligations that vanished from everyone else's memory but yours. Stress is Intelligence-suited and worth 0, so under ruff-only trump an Intelligence approach holding none of the led suit makes it trump. Accumulated pressure becomes ammunition, but every Stress must be played before cleanup or the ledger makes its holder answer for the erased balance.

> *A dark leather ledger; write the name of someone you owe, and they forget the debt but will never bargain with you again.*
> Wake: Write the name of someone you truly owe, then close the ledger before the ink dries.
> When: Your approach is Intelligence.
> Effect: After the deal, take all Stress from your draw pile and discard into hand. This is not drawing, so Stress does not duplicate. Shuffle the draw pile.
> *You remember every erased debt in the creditor's voice.*
> Reveal its price.
> Price: At cleanup, gain 1 Injury if any Stress remains in hand.
> *Every debt erased from another's memory remains written beneath your name.*
> *Your fingertips darken, and every promise sounds like a sum being read aloud.*

The drawing exception prevents the old multiplication bug: moved Stress neither triggers its own draw rule nor explicitly generates another copy. The Intelligence gate makes the Stress/trump interaction intentional. The Injury Price forces the player to spend every obligation they called due rather than sitting safely on a bloated hand.

## wake-lantern — the dead become part of you

The lantern no longer creates its dead hand from the bearer's reserve. When it wakes, it removes exactly seven cards from the world deck and holds them face up beneath the item card until invoked. They remain visible and outside the world deck across checks. Invocation moves that fixed hand into play, regardless of the current check's size, while the dead takes spectral form and plays beside the bearer.

> *A blackened brass lantern whose cold flame gives a captured spirit form.*
> Wake: With no cards tucked, catch a final breath in its chimney or coax in a lingering spirit. Tuck the top 7 world cards face up beneath it, outside the deck until invoked.
> Effect: Before drawing, move the tucked cards into a dead-hand row. It sits after you, follows the world's rule, uses your personal trump, and scores its wins for your goal. It may lead. When empty, it sits out and the world leads in its place.
> *The spirit stands beside you, aiding your work with the skills they carried in life.*
> Reveal its price.

At cleanup, all seven world cards return to the world deck, but exact counterparts move from the bearer's reserve into their discard. The spirit has joined the bearer mechanically as well as fictionally. Each trick it won also adds a status matching its winning card's printed suit, so that part of the price scales with the aid it provided. In a check longer than seven tricks, the dead hand sits out after playing its final card; in a shorter check, its unplayed cards still integrate at cleanup.

> Price: At cleanup, for each dead-hand card, move an ordinary card of the same suit and printed value from your reserve to your discard, if available, then return the dead hand to the world deck. For each trick it won, gain 1 status matching the printed suit of its winning card.
> Price: At cleanup, every living person but you forgets the invoked dead. Their spirit remains within you and cannot pass on until you die.
> *Their belongings, likenesses, and written records remain, belonging to someone no one remembers.*
> *Sometimes you hear their voice among your thoughts.*

Complete forgetting is the lasting fictional price. Material evidence remains: photographs show a stranger, letters bear an unknown signature, and mourners find themselves gathered around a coffin for someone they cannot name. The bearer alone remembers and accumulates the dead within their deck. A spirit whose matching card is unavailable still remains within the bearer; the reserve limit only prevents gaining another mechanical copy.

## hushbell-chain — invert the trigger, commit to the deafness

The bells do not merely conceal movement. Anyone who hears them can hear nothing else until they stop. In a check, that means committing a card before the lead and losing the ability to respond to the led suit or later plays:

> *A brass bell-chain; anyone who hears it hears nothing else until it stops.*
> Wake: Jingle it while people in earshot listen for something else.
> When: Your approach is Dexterity.
> Effect: After a trick but the last, you may ring the chain. Before the next lead, everyone who hears it sets 1 card face down; an affected world sets its leftmost. Reveal each on its owner's turn. It is legal, and a follower counts as unable to follow suit. This repeats.
> *Afterward, voices reach you as movement without sound.*
> Reveal its price.

Precommitment is the mechanical deafness. Affected followers may play off suit and keep personal trump live, but must choose without knowing the lead or any later play. The world shares the risk and commits its leftmost card. Who can hear the chain is a fictional fact: distance, walls, deafness, and deliberate ear protection all matter.

The Effect repeats, so the Price scales with actual use:

> Price: At cleanup, gain 1 Stumble for each time you rang the chain.
> *Your footsteps begin to ring like small bells, then sometimes make no sound at all.*
> *With each use, more of your speech becomes ringing or silence.*

The reveal makes the damage progressive and outward. The chain first replaces footsteps, then speech, with its own ringing or with nothing at all. Other people can hear the loss before the wearer understands how much of their voice is gone.

## iron-apple — a permanent floor, not limitless multiplication

The apple now makes the character physically enormous rather than treating permanent growth as an unexplained card multiplier:

> *An apple the colour and weight of old iron, slightly too heavy to be fruit.*
> Wake: Consume the entire apple in one sitting. Do not stop. Leave nothing but the seeds.
> Effect: You permanently grow to twice your height, with strength in proportion. When your approach is Strength, your Strength cards below 5 count as 5 when determining the trick winner.
> *You can no longer make yourself small, light, or gentle.*
> Reveal its price.

Doubling could create values of 16–18 against a world capped at 10, forever. A floor of 5 strengthens the character's weakest efforts without raising the game's ceiling. It can also make an unwanted win harder to avoid and deny the Brace that a low Strength loss would have supplied. The character has gained force, not finer control over it.

Twice the height is the effect outside checks. The character can reach, lift, carry, break, and intimidate at that scale, while buildings, vehicles, furniture, concealment, delicate work, and weak floors become concrete problems. The ordinary approach rule is enough to adjudicate those advantages and restrictions.

> Price: Gain 1 Injury, 1 Stumble, 1 Stress, and 4 Iron Apple Seeds, then remove this card from the game.
> *Your bones split and lengthen. Your skin closes only after the growth is done.*
> *Nothing made to hold you fits, and nothing made to bear you was built for your weight.*

The mixed statuses divide the transformation across bodily damage, unfamiliar balance, and the shock of inhabiting a world built at the wrong scale. One Injury can combine dangerously with existing wounds but does not automatically force the three-trick survival crisis caused by the old price. The Stumble and self-replicating Stress keep the initial transformation consequential after the wound is rested away. The permanent size remains the real price.

## dying-ring — the wish changes one present fact

The hand rebuild no longer stands in for wish fulfilment. Spending a charge literally changes one present fact about one person, object, or place in the current scene. The GM makes the smallest change that satisfies the exact words. The wish cannot alter the ring or escape a revealed Price.

> *A dark ring that frosts the wearer's breath while charged.*
> Wake: Hold it to dying lips for their last wish. Fulfill it after they die; the ring gains 1 charge.
> Effect: Spend 1 charge and wish about 1 person, object, or place here. The GM makes the smallest present change that fulfills it; it cannot alter the ring or a Price. After the deal, also shuffle and discard your hand, discard your draw pile top first, then take N cards from discard as your new hand.
> *Everyone remembers the change, but not a world before it.*
> Reveal its price.

Outside a check, the wish changes the fiction directly and the Price applies immediately. After the deal, the same change occurs and the wearer also rebuilds their hand from every card currently available in discard. The card selection accompanies the wish; it is not the wish's fictional effect.

> Price: At cleanup, gain 3 Injuries.
> *It was your dying wish.*
> *Your breath shortens and your skin grows cold.*

The old three-Curse price had no specified compulsion and could amplify an unrelated existing compulsion. Three Injuries make every wish an immediate threat to the wearer's survival under the core rules. Charges have no cap: each requires a death, a spoken final wish, and its fulfilment without the ring's help.

## halved-locket — the mind travels, the body keeps its cards

The locket explicitly exchanges minds. Each mind uses the complete personal card pool belonging to its borrowed body, so Injuries, deck composition, reserve, discard, and Curse compulsion stay with that body. Before an exchange with an NPC or anyone else who has no personal card pool, the GM builds one for that body. Changes suffered during the exchange remain with the body when the minds return.

The halves work at any distance. Simultaneous contact is the Wake; they never need to be brought together.

> *A locket broken in half. Each half reflects the face of whoever holds the other.*
> Wake: At the same moment, each holder grips a half over their heart and holds it until both grow hot.
> Effect: Between checks, the holders exchange minds. Each mind uses the other body's complete personal card pool, including reserve and discard. If a body has none, the GM builds one before the exchange. Repeat the Wake to exchange them again.
> *Your borrowed body reaches for what it loves and recoils from what it fears before you know why.*
> Reveal its price.

Every exchange, including a return, increases the shared count. Both current holders gain that much Stress, making it tempting to remain in the wrong bodies rather than pay to return.

> Price: Both holders gain 1 Stress for each mind exchange the locket has caused.
> *Each exchange leaves habits, memories, and desires behind in the body you leave.*
> *With every return, your body recognizes you less readily.*

# New trinkets — brainstorm

One per suit, each opening space nothing touches. The existing ten cluster hard: four value boosts, three hand or deck manipulation, two world-card degradation.

### Bite Strap — Strength · opens consolations

> *A strip of thick leather, bitten through in the same four places by four different people.*
> Wake: Bite down and make no sound while something is done to you.
> When: Your approach is Strength.
> Effect: The next trick you lose, take two consolations of different suits.
> Cost: Gain 1 Injury.

Strength plants its feet and eats the blow; consolations reward losing. Makes losing a trick something you might choose, which nothing else does. "Different suits" is deliberate — Braces never stack. **Weakest on the three-use test:** outside a check it is only proof that four other people endured something. Solve that before it goes in.

### Slip Knot — Dexterity · opens turn order

> *A length of cord tied in a knot that holds against any pull but falls apart at a touch.*
> Wake: Tie it around your wrist before drawing, and do not tighten it.
> When: Your approach is Dexterity.
> Effect: Once per check, play after everyone else in a trick. You may ignore follow-suit on that play.
> Cost: Gain 1 Stumble.

Seat position alone is worth **0.08 tricks** — not an item. The follow-suit rider is what makes it real, turning your trump live in a trick you chose. Overlaps Reposition, but that is only available when you lose; this is on demand.

### Fair Copy — Intelligence · opens discard order, and the survival scan

> *A second copy of a document in a neater hand, with one detail quietly different.*
> Wake: Copy out something true, and change one detail as you write.
> When: Your approach is Intelligence.
> Effect: Between checks, move 1 card from your discard to the bottom of the pile. This is the one exception to never rearranging discard.
> Cost: Gain 1 Stress.

Falsifying the record of your own wounds. *Moving* rather than reordering, because the scan counts Injuries in the top N and shuffling inside that window changes nothing. Two decisions first: it overrides a stated RULES.md rule, so the card must say so; and it partially defuses the death spiral, which is the GM's lethality dial.

### Sin Eater's Spoon — Weird · opens transferring harm

> *A wooden spoon worn thin on one side, smelling faintly of someone else's kitchen.*
> Wake: Eat a meal that was set aside for someone who is suffering.
> When: Your approach is Weird.
> Effect: Between checks, move 1 status from another player's discard into your own.
> Cost: Gain 1 Stress.

The only way one character takes another's damage. Tiny footprint, enormous weight at the table. Timing should probably be on the card: a survival requirement locks when it triggers, so the spoon only helps *before* a scan.

# Resolved

All ten trinkets implemented, exported, overflow-checked. The shipped wording is on the cards; this keeps only why each changed.

| Item | Change | Why |
|---|---|---|
| black-wedge | Draw 1 card → halve an unplayed world card, paying by halving a card of that suit in hand | **Reopened — see the rebuild above.** Degrading the obstacle was right; the same-suit payment was justified by a category error and barely constrained anything |

| red-thread | Discard cost dropped; "not in your hand for any purpose, including following suit" added | Nets zero cards, as it should. The void it manufactures is the point and had to be legible |
